## What Is a REC

A **REC (Renewable Energy Certificate)** certifies that a certain amount of electricity was genuinely produced from renewable sources. Typically, one REC equals one megawatt-hour (MWh) of renewable generation.

This mechanism separates the "electricity" from the "green attribute" of that electricity, so an organisation using grid power can buy RECs to claim clean-energy use under international standards — without installing its own generation.

## What RECs Are For and Who Uses Them

RECs serve as evidence of renewable-energy use in sustainability standards and initiatives such as **RE100**, and in requirements from parent companies or overseas trading partners. Organisations with clean-energy targets therefore buy RECs alongside normal electricity use, to show the renewable share in their ESG reporting.

In Thailand, buyers of green power under the **UGT (Utility Green Tariff)** also receive a renewable-energy certificate with their contract, which is why UGT and RECs go hand in hand.

## How a REC Differs from a Carbon Credit

Both relate to sustainability, but they are different instruments. A carbon credit is a right to a tonne of CO₂ emissions reduction, while a REC is evidence of the origin of renewable electricity, measured in MWh. An organisation claiming 100% clean electricity uses RECs to prove the share of energy — not carbon credits.

## Why RECs Must Reference Meter Data

Issuing a REC requires verifying the actual renewable energy produced, which comes from generation meters at the point of production. Meter accuracy is therefore a condition of the whole REC system's credibility. The meters used must be high-accuracy and calibrated to standard, so the certificate can genuinely be referenced and pass the issuing body's verification.
